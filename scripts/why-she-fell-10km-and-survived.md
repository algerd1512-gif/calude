# Why A Woman Fell 10 Kilometers From The Sky — And Survived

**Structure used:** Story / Character · **Target runtime:** ~13 min · **Word count:** ~2,270

> **Note on the title:** the idea tracker listed this as "a man," but the verified record holder for the highest fall ever survived without a parachute is a woman, Vesna Vulović. The script below tells her story accurately. If the channel wants to keep a gender-neutral title for the thumbnail, something like *"Why Someone Fell 10km From The Sky And Survived"* or *"She Fell 10km With No Parachute — And Lived"* both work; see the packaging skill for final title/thumbnail testing.

---

Somewhere above northern Czechoslovakia, on a freezing afternoon in January 1972, a piece of an airplane is falling out of a clear sky. Not the whole plane. A section of it, maybe a third of the fuselage, tumbling end over end. It started its fall higher than the summit of Mount Everest. Stack twelve Burj Khalifas, the tallest building on Earth, one on top of the other, and the drop would still start above the roof of the twelfth one.

It is the same altitude you're at if you've ever flown long-haul and looked out the window at a thin white line of clouds far below. Inside that piece is a 22-year-old flight attendant. She is unconscious. She has no parachute. The air around her is close to minus forty degrees Celsius, and there is barely enough oxygen in it to keep a person alive, let alone conscious. This is roughly the altitude commercial jets cruise at, chosen partly because the air is thin enough up there to cut fuel-burning drag. It is not an altitude built for a human body to survive falling through. In a few minutes, she is going to hit the ground.

She should not survive this. Nobody should. And yet Vesna Vulović did. More than fifty years later, her fall is still the highest one any human has ever survived without a parachute. No one has come close to beating it since.

To understand how, you have to start a few weeks earlier, with a scheduling mistake.

— — —

Vulović worked for JAT Yugoslav Airlines, flying out of Belgrade. She'd been a flight attendant for less than two years. In January 1972, she wasn't even supposed to be on the flight that made her famous. There was another flight attendant at the airline with the same first name, and somewhere in the paperwork, the two of them got swapped.

Vulović ended up rostered onto a run from Stockholm back to Belgrade instead of her usual route. She wasn't upset about it. She'd never been to Scandinavia. The trip meant a stopover in Copenhagen and a night in a Sheraton hotel, the kind of hotel she'd always wanted to stay in. A clerical error had just handed her a nice week of work.

On January 26, the aircraft, a McDonnell Douglas DC-9, worked its way down through Scandinavia and Central Europe: Stockholm, then Copenhagen, then on toward Zagreb and Belgrade. By the time it left Copenhagen, there were 28 people aboard. Vulović was one of four crew.

Somewhere in the cargo hold, under the passenger cabin, sat a piece of luggage. Investigators later concluded it held a bomb.

Yugoslav authorities blamed Croatian nationalist extremists for planting it, this was an era when Yugoslav state airlines were repeatedly targeted by émigré separatist groups, though no one was ever arrested or convicted. What's certain is what the bomb did. As the DC-9 cruised near its assigned altitude, 33,330 feet, 10,160 meters, roughly two kilometers higher than Everest, the explosive detonated in the hold.

The aircraft didn't just crash. In an instant, the cabin depressurized, the fuselage buckled, and the plane tore into three separate pieces: the nose, the midsection with the wings, and the tail. Each piece then fell on its own, scattering wreckage across miles of the Czechoslovak countryside below. It would take rescue teams working through the winter darkness to even locate all three crash sites.

— — —

Twenty-seven of the twenty-eight people on board died, most of them on impact with the ground below. Vulović was in the tail section, near the galley. When the fuselage tore apart, that section, with her still inside it, began falling toward Czechoslovakia on its own.

She wasn't the first person in history to fall from an aircraft with no parachute and live. During the Second World War, a Soviet airman named Ivan Chisov fell around 7,000 meters after bailing out of a burning bomber and survived, badly broken but alive. An American gunner, Alan Magee, fell 6,700 meters from a crippled B-17 the following year and crashed through the glass roof of a French train station. A British tail gunner, Nicholas Alkemade, jumped from a burning plane at 5,500 meters with no parachute at all and walked away with bruises. Each of those was treated as close to a miracle.

Vulović fell nearly twice as far as any of them.

To see just how strange that number is, it helps to zoom back down to ground level first. Safety researchers generally put the point where an ordinary fall becomes a coin-flip between life and death at around 15 meters, roughly four stories. Past about 30 meters, ten stories, survival is already rare. Past that, it keeps dropping toward zero the higher you go.

Vulović fell 10,160 meters. That's not ten stories. That's more than three thousand.

So here's the puzzle at the center of this story: how do you fall from an altitude airliners cruise at, and land alive?

Start with the air itself. At 10,000 meters, there isn't enough oxygen to stay conscious for long, and the cabin had just violently depressurized. Investigators believe Vulović likely blacked out within seconds. Not from the fall. From the thin, freezing air rushing in. Doctors later pointed to something else, too: she had unusually low blood pressure, which may have sped up how quickly she lost consciousness.

That sounds like bad news. It wasn't. A body that's gone limp survives violent impact better than a body that's braced and tense. It's the same reason drunk drivers sometimes walk away from crashes that kill sober passengers sitting right next to them. Vulović was falling through the sky completely unconscious, which meant every muscle in her body was slack.

Second factor: how she was falling. Not head-first, not like a skydiver. She was still sealed inside a chunk of the aircraft's fuselage, roughly a third of a jet, and pinned into her seat area by a food and drinks trolley that had jammed against her when the plane broke apart. A lone human body falling from that height reaches a terminal velocity of around 200 kilometers per hour, close to 125 miles per hour, roughly the top speed of a high-speed passenger train. A tumbling, irregular slab of airplane fuselage doesn't fall like that. It catches air unevenly as it spins, the way a leaf falls slower than a stone dropped from the same height. That extra drag wasn't enough on its own to make the landing survivable. But it mattered.

Third factor, and the one nobody could have engineered: where it came down. The wreckage fell onto a steep, heavily wooded, snow-covered hillside near the village of Srbská Kamenice. The trees and the deep snowpack absorbed and spread out the force of the impact, the way a mattress works compared to a concrete floor. The slope did the rest, letting the fuselage skid and slide down the hillside instead of stopping dead in an instant.

Take away any one of those three things, the blackout, the tumbling fuselage, the snow-covered slope, and this is a different story. All three lined up, on the same afternoon, over the same patch of forest, for the same person.

— — —

A local villager named Bruno Honke heard screaming near his home that evening. He followed the sound through the snow and the trees until he found it: a torn-open section of an airplane, half-buried in the hillside, with a woman still alive inside it. He'd had first-aid training decades earlier, during the war, and he used it now, clearing her airway and keeping her stable until rescue crews could reach the remote crash site.

By the time he reached her, she had already been lying in the snow, in sub-zero temperatures, for some time. What he found was a young woman with a fractured skull, three broken vertebrae, both legs broken, several broken ribs, and a shattered pelvis. She was rushed to a hospital and slipped into a coma. It lasted 27 days.

When she woke up, she remembered nothing. Not the bomb, not the fall, not the landing. To this day, no one, including Vulović herself, has ever been able to describe what those few minutes actually felt like from the inside.

She was paralyzed from the waist down for months, and doctors weren't sure she would ever walk again. Over the following year and a half in the hospital, she went through repeated surgeries and slow, painful physical therapy just to relearn how to move her own legs. Then, roughly ten months after the crash, she stood up, and walked. The airline's doctors later ruled her medically unfit to work as cabin crew again. She never flew for a living after that.

— — —

You'd think that would be the end of the story. It's actually where a second mystery opens up.

For decades, the official account held: bomb, explosion at cruising altitude, catastrophic fall, one survivor. Then in 2009, the German current-affairs program Tagesschau aired an investigation by journalists Peter Hornung and Pavel Theiner, built on newly surfaced Czech aviation documents. Their claim was startling: the plane, they said, hadn't broken up at 33,000 feet at all. They argued it had actually been shot down by mistake, hit by a Czechoslovak Air Force jet that mistook it for an enemy aircraft during a botched forced landing, at an altitude of only a few hundred meters. Local eyewitnesses, they said, recalled seeing the plane still intact, low under the clouds, moments before it went down. If true, the record that made Vulović famous would have been built on a cover story.

It was a serious enough claim that investigators went back to the original evidence: the flight recorders pulled from the wreckage in 1972. Specialists from Yugoslavia, Czechoslovakia, and the Netherlands re-examined the black box data: altitude, speed, acceleration, the exact moment of the explosion. Their conclusion held firm. The aircraft really had broken apart at roughly 10,160 meters, just as the original investigation found. The low-altitude theory didn't match the recorded flight data, and Czech aviation authorities dismissed it.

So the record stood. In 1985, thirteen years after the crash, Guinness World Records formally credited Vulović with the highest fall ever survived without a parachute: 10,160 meters. The award was presented to her in London, handed over by, of all people, Paul McCartney.

One mystery was closed. The other one, who actually built the bomb, stayed open for decades. A day after the crash, a man calling himself a Croatian nationalist phoned a Swedish newspaper, Kvällsposten, and claimed responsibility. No one was ever arrested. It took until 2024 for the story to move again, when the Swedish investigative program Kalla fakta aired new reporting built on declassified Yugoslav security service files. The documents pointed to a group of seven exiled Croatian nationalists living in Sweden and Germany at the time, part of a network known as the Bugojno group. More than fifty years on, nobody involved has ever stood trial.

— — —

Grounded from flying, Vulović took an office job with JAT instead. At home, she became something close to a celebrity. A folk singer, Miroslav Ilić, released a song about her, "Vesna the Stewardess." Strangers recognized her on the street for the rest of her life, as the woman who fell out of the sky and lived.

Then, in the 1990s, that same fame gave her something most ordinary citizens in Serbia didn't have: a public platform. She joined the opposition Democratic Party against Slobodan Milošević's government, and turned up at street protests that regularly ended in clashes with riot police. The state-owned airline forced her into early retirement, punishment for her politics. She was never arrested. The government, it turned out, didn't want the headlines that jailing "the woman who fell from the sky" would bring.

She kept at it through the Bulldozer Revolution of October 2000, the uprising that finally forced Milošević from power, and stayed politically active for years afterward, campaigning for Serbia's entry into the European Union.

For all the attention the fall brought her, Vulović never described herself as lucky. In a 2012 interview, decades after the crash, she put it plainly: "Whenever I think of the accident, I have a prevailing, grave feeling of guilt for surviving it, and I cry. Then I think maybe I should not have survived at all." Twenty-seven people did not get to walk away from that hillside. She never stopped being aware of that.

She died in Belgrade in December 2016, at 66 years old.

More than fifty years after that January afternoon, nobody has fallen from higher and lived. It isn't a record anyone is trying to break. Remember the number from earlier: a fall of 30 meters is already close to unsurvivable. Vulović fell 10,160. And she is still, to this day, the only person who has ever come back from a fall anywhere near that far.

That's really what this story comes down to. Not one lucky break. A short, narrow chain of them, a scheduling mix-up, a blackout, a jammed food trolley, a snow-covered hillside, each one on its own doing almost nothing. Together, they did the impossible.

That piece of fuselage is still tumbling out of a clear January sky, higher than Everest, higher than a stack of twelve Burj Khalifas. For everyone else who has ever fallen that far, that image ends only one way. For one flight attendant on one ordinary Wednesday in 1972, it didn't.

If stories like this, where the odds should have ended everything and somehow didn't, are the kind of thing you want more of, subscribe, because there are others just as strange still to come.
