It started its fall higher than the summit of Mount Everest.

The air around her is close to minus forty degrees Celsius,

This is roughly the altitude commercial jets cruise at,

It is not an altitude built for a human body to survive falling through.
In a few minutes, she is going to hit the ground.
She should not survive this. Nobody should. And yet Vesna Vulovic did.
More than fifty years later, her fall is still the highest one any human has ever survived without a parachute.
No one has come close to beating it since.
To understand how, you have to start a few weeks earlier, with a scheduling mistake.
Vulovic had wanted to be a flight attendant for a simple reason.
She wanted to see the world, and London most of all, home of her favorite band, the Beatles.
There was just one problem.
Her blood pressure was chronically low, low enough that she failed the medical exam the airline required.
So before her second attempt, she drank as much coffee as she could stomach,
spiked her blood pressure just enough, and passed.
It's a small, funny detail. It will matter again later, in a way that isn't funny at all.
She got the job with JAT Yugoslav Airlines, flying out of Belgrade.
Eight months in, in January 1972, she wasn't even supposed to be on the flight that made her famous.
A colleague, also named Vesna, was scheduled for a Stockholm to Belgrade run.
Somewhere in the paperwork, the airline mixed the two of them up and rostered Vulovic instead.
She wasn't upset about it. She'd never been to Scandinavia.
The trip meant a stopover in Copenhagen and a night in a Sheraton hotel,
the kind of hotel she'd always wanted to stay in.
A clerical error had just handed her a nice week of work.
On January 26, the aircraft, a McDonnell Douglas DC 9, worked its way down through Scandinavia and Central Europe.
Stockholm, then a layover in Copenhagen in the early afternoon, then on toward Zagreb and Belgrade.
As passengers filed off during the stopover, Vulovic noticed one man behaving oddly,
peeling off toward a different exit instead of leaving with everyone else.
She'd remember that detail for the rest of her life.
By the time the DC 9 left Copenhagen a little after three p m, there were twenty eight people aboard.
Vulovic was one of four crew.
Somewhere in the cargo hold, under the passenger cabin, sat a piece of luggage.
Investigators later concluded it held a bomb.
Yugoslav authorities blamed Croatian nationalist extremists for planting it.
This was an era when Yugoslav state airlines were repeatedly targeted by emigre separatist groups,
though no one was ever arrested or convicted.
What's certain is what the bomb did.
As the DC 9 cruised near its assigned altitude, thirty three thousand three hundred thirty feet,
ten thousand one hundred sixty meters, roughly two kilometers higher than Everest,
the explosive detonated in the hold.
The aircraft didn't just crash. In an instant, the cabin depressurized, the fuselage buckled,
and the plane tore into three separate pieces. The nose, the midsection with the wings, and the tail.
Each piece then fell on its own, scattering wreckage across miles of the Czechoslovak countryside below.
It would take rescue teams working through the winter darkness to even locate all three crash sites.
Twenty seven of the twenty eight people on board died, most of them on impact with the ground below.
Vulovic was in the tail section, near the galley.
When the fuselage tore apart, that section, with her still inside it, began falling toward Czechoslovakia on its own.
She wasn't the first person in history to fall from an aircraft with no parachute and live.
During the Second World War, a Soviet airman named Ivan Chisov fell around seven thousand meters
after bailing out of a burning bomber and survived, badly broken but alive.
An American gunner, Alan Magee, fell six thousand seven hundred meters from a crippled B seventeen
and crashed through the glass roof of a French train station.
A British tail gunner, Nicholas Alkemade, jumped from a burning plane at five thousand five hundred meters
with no parachute at all and walked away with bruises.
Each of those was treated as close to a miracle.
Vulovic fell nearly twice as far as any of them.
To see just how strange that number is, it helps to zoom back down to ground level first.
Safety researchers generally put the point where an ordinary fall becomes a coin flip between life and death,
at around fifteen meters, roughly four stories.
Past about thirty meters, ten stories, survival is already rare.
Past that, it keeps dropping toward zero the higher you go.
Vulovic fell ten thousand one hundred sixty meters. That's not ten stories.
That's more than three thousand.
So here's the puzzle at the center of this story.
How do you fall from an altitude airliners cruise at, and land alive?
Start with the air itself. At ten thousand meters, there isn't enough oxygen to stay conscious for long,
and the cabin had just violently depressurized.
Investigators believe Vulovic likely blacked out within seconds. Not from the fall.
From the thin, freezing air rushing in.
And here's where that low blood pressure comes back in,
the same condition she'd once had to fake her way past with a cup of coffee just to get the job.
Doctors believe it likely sped up how quickly she lost consciousness once the cabin depressurized.
That sounds like bad news. It wasn't.
A body that's gone limp survives violent impact better than a body that's braced and tense.
It's the same reason drunk drivers sometimes walk away from crashes that kill sober passengers sitting right next to them.
Vulovic was falling through the sky completely unconscious, which meant every muscle in her body was slack.
Second factor. How she was falling. Not head first, not like a skydiver.
She was still sealed inside a chunk of the aircraft's fuselage, roughly a third of a jet,
and pinned into her seat area by a food and drinks trolley
that had jammed against her when the plane broke apart.
A lone human body falling from that height reaches a terminal velocity
of around two hundred kilometers per hour, close to one hundred twenty five miles per hour,
roughly the top speed of a high speed passenger train.
A tumbling, irregular slab of airplane fuselage doesn't fall like that.
It catches air unevenly as it spins,
the way a leaf falls slower than a stone dropped from the same height.
That extra drag wasn't enough on its own to make the landing survivable. But it mattered.
Third factor, and the one nobody could have engineered. Where it came down.
The wreckage fell onto a steep, heavily wooded, snow covered hillside near the village of Srbska Kamenice.
The trees and the deep snowpack absorbed and spread out the force of the impact,
the way a mattress works compared to a concrete floor.
The slope did the rest, letting the fuselage skid and slide down the hillside
instead of stopping dead in an instant.
Take away any one of those three things, the blackout, the tumbling fuselage, the snow covered slope,
and this is a different story. All three lined up, on the same afternoon,
over the same patch of forest, for the same person.
A local villager named Bruno Honke heard screaming near his home that evening.
He followed the sound through the snow and the trees until he found it.
A torn open section of an airplane, half buried in the hillside, with a woman still alive inside it.
He'd had first aid training decades earlier, during the war,
and he used it now, clearing her airway
and keeping her stable until rescue crews could reach the remote crash site.
By the time he reached her, she had already been lying in the snow,
in sub zero temperatures, for some time.
What he found was a young woman with a fractured skull, three broken vertebrae,
both legs broken, several broken ribs, and a shattered pelvis.
She was rushed to a hospital, where, by her own later account,
doctors considered her clinically dead for a period of hours before she stabilized.
She then slipped into a coma. It lasted twenty seven days.
When she woke up, she remembered nothing. Not the bomb, not the fall, not the landing.
To this day, no one, including Vulovic herself,
has ever been able to describe what those few minutes

She was paralyzed from the waist down for months, and doctors weren't sure she would ever walk again.
Over the following year and a half in the hospital,

Then, roughly ten months after the crash, she stood up, and walked.
The airline's doctors later ruled her medically unfit to work as cabin crew again.
She never flew for a living after that.

It's actually where a second mystery opens up.
For decades, the official account held. Bomb, explosion at cruising altitude, catastrophic fall, one survivor.

It was a serious enough claim that investigators went back to the original evidence.

The aircraft really had broken apart at roughly ten thousand one hundred sixty meters,

The low altitude theory didn't match the recorded flight data, and Czech aviation authorities dismissed it.
So the record stood. In 1985, thirteen years after the crash,

The award was presented to her in London,

Grounded from flying, Vulovic took an office job with JAT instead.
